Figure 1
Public charging and energy inputs lead to a 2023 cohort of 12 sites and a
2024 holdout using the same sites and equipment sizes.

Figure 2
The two cohorts yield 70 matched site-week pairs. Offline comparisons use both
years, while rolling dispatch uses 2024 sessions with forecasts trained in 2023.

Figure 3
Energy flows from the grid and photovoltaics to the station bus, from the bus
to vehicles, and in either direction between the bus and the stationary battery.

Figure 4
Illustrative immediate and deferred charging curves deliver the same final
energy. Two brackets at the early checkpoint show the intrinsic shortfall and
the additional gap from deferring charge.

Figure 5
Charging timelines illustrate how a battery can store PV surplus before arrival
for immediate charging, and how flexible charging can shift vehicle demand to a
later low-carbon interval.

Figure 6
The offline experiment either minimizes carbon at fixed early readiness or
minimizes avoidable shortfall at a fixed carbon ceiling. Both comparisons
require full energy delivery within the operating budget.

Figure 7
The controller plans up to 24 hours and executes the first two. The next
decision carries forward delivered energy and battery state of charge.

Figure 8
Scatterplot of the decline in incremental storage carbon benefit when charging
can use the full session window. Most of the 70 paired points exceed one gram
per delivered kilowatt-hour in both 2023 and 2024.

Figure 9
The median and interquartile range of the storage carbon benefit are shown at
three photovoltaic sizes. Benefits are larger with the 50-kilowatt-hour
battery than with the 12.5-kilowatt-hour battery in both years.

Figure 10
Four lines show no-battery early energy shortfall for two checkpoints and
two years. Shortfall rises toward the strictest carbon target. The dotted
line shows the maximum shortfall across the battery sizes, years and checkpoints.
